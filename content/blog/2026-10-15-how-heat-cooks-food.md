**TL;DR:** Heat moves by conduction, convection and radiation. Dry heat (searing, roasting, frying) browns and builds flavor; moist heat (boiling, steaming, braising) is gentle and tenderizes. Don't crowd the pan, rest meat after cooking, and judge doneness with a thermometer.

## Three ways heat travels

- **Conduction** — direct contact. A hot pan heats the steak touching it; the outside of the steak heats the inside.
- **Convection** — moving air or liquid carries heat. Boiling water, a convection oven, deep frying.
- **Radiation** — energy that travels through space. A broiler, glowing coals, a toaster. Microwave ovens use a different kind of electromagnetic energy that heats the water inside food.

Most methods combine more than one. Recognizing which dominates helps you predict how food will behave.

## Dry-heat methods

No water used for transferring heat, so temperatures can climb well above boiling.

- **Sauté and stir-fry** — high heat, small pieces, little fat, constant motion.
- **Sear** — very hot pan, food left alone to form a crust.
- **Roast and bake** — surrounding hot air in an oven.
- **Grill and broil** — intense radiant heat from below or above.
- **Deep and shallow frying** — hot fat, which is dry heat despite being a liquid.

## Moist-heat methods

Water and steam can't exceed about 212°F (100°C) at sea level, so these methods are gentler.

- **Boil** — vigorous bubbles; for pasta and sturdy vegetables.
- **Simmer** — small, lazy bubbles; for stocks, soups, and most sauces.
- **Poach** — barely moving water; for eggs, fish, and fruit.
- **Steam** — food above, not in, the water. Keeps nutrients and texture.
- **Braise and stew** — brown first, then cook slowly, partly or fully covered in liquid. Ideal for tough, cheap cuts, whose connective tissue turns to gelatin over time.

## Browning is flavor

When protein- and sugar-rich foods get hot and dry, the **Maillard reaction** creates hundreds of new flavor compounds — the crust on bread, the sear on meat, toasted nuts. **Caramelization** is sugar breaking down on its own, as in onions cooked slowly to a deep brown.

To brown well:

- Pat food dry. Surface water must evaporate before browning can begin.
- Preheat the pan.
- **Don't crowd the pan.** Too much food drops the temperature and releases steam, so food stews instead of searing. Cook in batches.
- Leave it alone until it releases from the pan on its own.

## Carryover cooking and resting

Large pieces of meat keep cooking after you take them off the heat as heat moves from the hot exterior inward — often by several degrees. Remove them a little before the target temperature and let them rest. Resting also lets juices settle, so less ends up on the board.

## Doneness

Times in recipes are estimates. Thickness, starting temperature, pan material and your stove all change the result. An instant-read thermometer turns guessing into knowing, and for meat, poultry and eggs it is also your safety check.

## Fat and smoke points

Every fat starts to smoke at some temperature. Smoking oil tastes acrid and is a step toward a fire. For high-heat searing, choose refined oils with higher smoke points; save butter and extra-virgin olive oil for moderate heat or finishing.

## Why this outlasts any technology

Induction hobs, combi ovens and devices yet to be invented all do the same three things: conduct, convect and radiate heat. Understand the physics and you can cook on anything. Tomorrow: seasoning and building flavor.
