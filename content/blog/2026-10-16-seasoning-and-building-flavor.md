**TL;DR:** Season in layers, not just at the end. Salt makes flavors louder, acid makes them brighter, fat carries them, and a little sweetness or bitterness creates balance. Taste with a clean spoon at every stage, and when something is flat, reach for salt or acid before anything else.

## Taste versus flavor

Your tongue detects a handful of basic tastes — **salty, sour, sweet, bitter and umami** (savory). Flavor is much bigger: it is taste plus aroma, texture and temperature. That's why food seems bland when you have a cold. Good cooking balances the basic tastes and builds aroma on top.

## Salt

Salt is the most important seasoning in the kitchen. It does not just make food salty; it suppresses bitterness and makes other flavors more vivid.

- **Season early and throughout.** Salt added during cooking penetrates; salt sprinkled at the end sits on the surface.
- **Salt pasta water** generously, since it's the only chance to season the pasta itself.
- **Salt meat ahead** — even 40 minutes, or up to a day in the fridge, seasons deeper and helps browning.
- **Use one salt consistently** so you learn its strength. Different salts vary widely in how salty a spoonful is.
- Add a little at a time. You can always add more; you can't take it out.

## Acid

Lemon, lime, vinegar, wine, tomatoes, yogurt. Acid brightens, cuts richness, and wakes up a dish that tastes heavy or muddy. A squeeze of citrus or a splash of vinegar at the end is the most common professional fix.

## Fat

Fat carries flavor, gives richness and texture, and many aromatic compounds dissolve in it. Butter, oils, cream, nuts and cheese all contribute. Too little and food is dry and thin; too much and it is greasy — balance with acid.

## Sweet, bitter, umami and heat

- **Sweetness** rounds off acidity and heat. A pinch of sugar in a tomato sauce is an old trick.
- **Bitterness** — coffee, dark greens, charred edges, cocoa — adds depth in small doses.
- **Umami** comes from aged cheese, mushrooms, tomatoes, soy sauce, fish sauce, and slow-cooked meat. It makes food taste savory and complete.
- **Heat** from chilies, pepper or mustard adds excitement. Add gradually; heat builds as a dish cooks and sits.

## Aromatics and spices

- **Aromatic vegetables** — onions, garlic, celery, carrots, peppers, ginger — gently cooked in fat at the start form the base of countless cuisines.
- **Whole spices** benefit from toasting in a dry pan until fragrant, then grinding.
- **Ground spices** bloom when briefly fried in oil.
- **Dried herbs** go in early; **fresh, tender herbs** like parsley, cilantro and basil go in at the end.
- Spices fade. Buy small amounts and store them away from heat and light.

## Tasting and fixing

Taste at every stage, always with a clean spoon. When a dish falls flat:

- **Bland** — add salt first.
- **Heavy or dull** — add acid.
- **Too salty** — dilute with more unsalted ingredients or liquid, or balance with acid and fat.
- **Too sour** — add a little sweetness or fat.
- **Too spicy** — add dairy, fat, sweetness or more of the base.
- **Missing depth** — add umami, or brown something.

## Why this outlasts any technology
