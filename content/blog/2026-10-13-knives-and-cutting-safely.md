**TL;DR:** Own a chef's knife, a paring knife and a serrated knife. Keep them sharp, anchor your board with a damp towel, hold food with a curled claw, and cut flat sides down. Never catch a falling knife, never leave one in a sink, and wash and dry it right away.

## The only three knives you need

- **Chef's knife** (20–25 cm or 8–10 inches) — does about 90% of the work: chopping, slicing, mincing.
- **Paring knife** — small, for peeling, trimming and in-hand work.
- **Serrated knife** — for bread, tomatoes, and anything with a hard outside and soft inside.

Buy for comfort and balance, not for the number of pieces in a block.

## Sharp is safer

A dull knife needs more force, skids off tomato skins and onion layers, and goes where you don't want it to. A sharp knife goes where you guide it.

- **Honing** with a steel realigns the edge. Do it often.
- **Sharpening** with a whetstone or a good sharpener removes metal to create a new edge. Do it when honing no longer brings the bite back.
- Test with a sheet of paper or a tomato skin: a sharp knife slices without tearing.

## Set up a stable station

- Put a damp towel or a non-slip mat under the board so it cannot slide.
- Use wood or plastic boards. Glass, stone and plates destroy edges and let the knife skate.
- Give yourself space. Clear clutter before you start.

## The grip and the claw

- **Knife hand** — pinch the blade just in front of the handle between thumb and the side of the index finger; wrap the other fingers around the handle. This gives control.
- **Guide hand** — curl your fingertips under like a claw, with the knuckles forward. The flat of the blade rests against the knuckles, and the fingertips stay safely behind.
- **Stability** — cut round foods in half first so they sit flat. A rolling onion is how accidents happen.
- **Motion** — let the knife do the work with a forward or rocking motion, not downward pressure.

## The basic cuts

- **Slice** — even, flat pieces.
- **Dice** — cubes; small, medium and large are roughly 6, 12 and 20 mm (¼, ½, ¾ inch).
- **Mince** — very fine pieces, for garlic, herbs and shallots.
- **Julienne** — thin matchsticks.
- **Chiffonade** — stack leaves, roll tightly, and slice into ribbons.

Even pieces cook evenly. That is the real reason the cuts exist.

## Knife safety rules

- **Never try to catch a falling knife.** Step back and let it fall.
- Never leave a knife in a sink of soapy water where nobody can see it.
- Carry it pointing down, blade turned back, close to your side, and say "knife behind" in a shared kitchen.
- Use the spine, not the edge, to scrape food across the board.
- Wash knives by hand, dry immediately, and store them in a block, on a magnetic strip, or with a blade guard — never loose in a drawer.

## If you cut yourself

Rinse with clean water, apply firm pressure with a clean cloth, raise the hand, and cover with a waterproof dressing. Seek medical care for deep cuts, cuts that won't stop bleeding, or anything that gapes. Discard any food that has blood on it.

## Why this outlasts any technology

Food processors and gadgets will keep promising to replace the knife. They never quite do. A good knife and steady hands are among the most durable skills a cook can have. Tomorrow: getting organized before the heat goes on.
